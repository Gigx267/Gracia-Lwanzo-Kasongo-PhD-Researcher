The Project

Scope & Contribution

International humanitarian law sets out clear obligations to protect civilians in armed conflict. Yet in eastern DRC — one of the world's longest-running and most complex humanitarian theatres — the gap between legal obligation and lived protection remains wide.

This doctoral project asks why. It traces how international legal norms are interpreted, negotiated, and operationalised by humanitarian actors, state institutions, and affected communities, and what that means for the design of protection policy.

The contribution is twofold: an empirically grounded account of humanitarian governance in the Great Lakes region, and a conceptual argument about how law functions as a practice — not merely a text — within humanitarian response.

Central claim: legal protection frameworks succeed or fail in the translation — between Geneva and Goma, between doctrine and decision.

Expected Contribution

  • Empirical: first-hand accounts of protection coordination across DRC and its borders.
  • Conceptual: a practice-based model of legal norm translation in humanitarian settings.
  • Policy: practical recommendations for protection programming and coordination.
Doctoral thesis · UCLouvain · expected completion 2025
02

Core Questions

Four interlocking questions organise the doctoral inquiry, moving from law to practice to policy.

  1. Q1

    How are international humanitarian law obligations interpreted by actors operating in eastern DRC, and which norms are treated as actionable in daily practice?

  2. Q2

    What governance arrangements — coordination mechanisms, mandates, funding structures — shape who is protected, where, and by whom?

  3. Q3

    Where and why does the translation from legal norm to protection outcome break down, and what strategies do practitioners use to bridge the gap?

  4. Q4

    What can comparative and contextual evidence from the Great Lakes contribute to broader debates on humanitarian governance and legal accountability?

03

Research Themes

The project sits at the intersection of law, governance, and conflict — with a regional anchor in Central Africa.

International Humanitarian Law

How legal norms — distinction, proportionality, precaution — are understood and applied by humanitarian and state actors in active conflict zones.

Humanitarian Governance

Coordination architecture, mandates, and the political economy of aid — and how these determine protection priorities on the ground.

Conflict & the Great Lakes

Regional dynamics in eastern DRC and cross-border humanitarian flows — armed group governance, displacement, and access negotiation.

Regional focus

Law, Policy & Practice

How legal norms become policy instruments — and how evidence from the field can inform more effective protection frameworks.

04

Methodology

Mixed Qualitative Design

Doctrinal Legal Analysis

Close reading of treaty texts, customary norms, UN Security Council resolutions, and humanitarian policy instruments.

Semi-Structured Interviews

Practitioners, policy actors, and community representatives — with informed consent and anonymisation protocols.

Field Observation

Contextual immersion in coordination meetings, field visits, and community-level protection structures.

Comparative Case Analysis

Structured comparison across sites and response contexts to identify patterns and variation.

Research Ethics & Positionality

Fieldwork involving conflict-affected populations follows strict ethical review, do-no-harm principles, and data protection standards. My own background in eastern DRC informs access and interpretation, and is treated as an object of critical reflection rather than an unexamined advantage.
